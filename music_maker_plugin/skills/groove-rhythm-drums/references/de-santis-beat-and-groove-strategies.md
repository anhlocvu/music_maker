# Beat Programming, Pocket & Kick-Bass Strategies (Dennis DeSantis, *Making Music*)

*Distilled faithfully from Dennis DeSantis, "Making Music: 74 Creative Strategies for Electronic Music Producers" (Part 2: Problems of Progressing, pp. 120–169).*

## 1. Rhythmic Engines & Loops

### 3+3+2 Rhythm / Tresillo (pp. 133–137)
- A 16th-note grouping of **3 + 3 + 2** (repeating every 2 beats) or half-time 8th-note **3 + 3 + 2** (every 4 beats).
- Most effective when **layered against symmetrical patterns** (e.g., steady 4/4 kick or 8th-note hats) so the asymmetrical hits pull against the grid before the final `"2"` resynchronizes with the downbeat.
- **Variations**:
  - Omit one hit (e.g., drop the middle `"3"`) for subtler syncopation, or ornament right before `"2"`.
  - **Extend the `"3"`**: Chain `3+3+3+3...` across a bar and pause before realigning on strong beats in bar 2 (creating strong rhythmic tension and release).
- Apply `3+3+2` not only to kicks, but also to basslines, chord stabs, and melodies.

### Asynchronous / Polyrhythmic Loops & Implied Rhythm (pp. 120–128)
- Trigger percussion or pitched instruments with **loops of different lengths** (e.g., 5-step or 7-step hi-hat/percussion cycle against a 16-step kick/snare bar) so phase shifts create continuously evolving patterns without writing dozens of bars by hand.
- Short non-zero-crossing or uneven loop lengths create **implied secondary rhythms** through rhythmic accents at the loop seam.

## 2. Programming Beats 1–4

### Programming Beats 1: On Looseness (pp. 138–143)
Electronic genres (techno, house, EDM) often want tight grid quantization and even velocity; organic/hip-hop/funk styles need human looseness:
1. **Partial Quantization (e.g., 50%)**: Pulls sloppy hits halfway toward the grid without erasing human feel.
2. **Selective Quantization**: Lock kick + snare tightly to the grid while leaving hi-hats/percussion unquantized—or quantize hats and leave snare/kick loose.
3. **Humanize / Subtle Randomization**: Apply small timing and velocity offsets *only after* parts are cleanly aligned on the grid (randomizing already-sloppy parts compounds errors).
4. **Velocity Contouring on Subdivisions**: On straight 16th-note hi-hats, accent the **1st and 3rd sixteenths** (`1, e, +, a` → strong, soft, medium, soft) to breathe life into a quantized pattern.
5. **Micro-Shifting Specific Elements**:
   - Push snare or hi-hats **slightly behind the beat** (late) for a heavy, laid-back funk/boom-bap pocket.
   - Pull hats **slightly ahead of the beat** (early) for a driving, urgent ska/punk energy.
   - Note: Micro-shifts only register perceptually when at least one reference element stays anchored on the grid.

### Programming Beats 2: Linear Drumming (pp. 144–148)
- **Human-limb ceiling**: Acoustic drummers have four limbs (max 4 simultaneous voices). Respecting that ceiling keeps programmed drums realistic.
- **Linear Drumming**: Strictly **monophonic** drum writing—**no two kit pieces hit at the exact same time** (analogous to melodic hocket).
- Treats kick, snare, hats, and toms as equal participants in one composite groove rather than fixed "timekeeper vs. backbeat" strata.
- Playing continuous 16th-note linear patterns at fast tempos (~170 BPM, e.g., drum and bass) with strong velocity accents creates a rolling, quasi-melodic composite line.

### Programming Beats 3: Ghost Notes (pp. 149–152)
- **Ghost notes** are ultra-quiet snare (or percussion) hits placed between primary accents (e.g., 2nd/3rd sixteenths of beats 1 and 3) to propel forward motion.
- Also effective as rapid **32nd-note pickups** anticipating the downbeat kick, optionally dragged slightly behind the beat.
- Require velocity-sensitive samples/presets (if a kit has flat velocity response, ghost notes sound like full accents and break the groove).

### Programming Beats 4: Top, Bottom, Left, Right (pp. 153–157)
- **Top vs. Bottom Architecture**:
  - *Top half* = cymbals (hi-hat, ride, crashes) — higher physical position and high frequency band.
  - *Bottom half* = shells (kick, snare, toms) — lower physical position and low/mid frequency band.
  - **Top-Down genres (Jazz)**: Ride cymbal + foot hi-hat establish the primary timekeeping grid; kick and snare comment/interrupt asymmetrically.
  - **Bottom-Up genres (Rock, Pop, R&B, Hip-Hop)**: Kick and snare form the defining grid; cymbals provide secondary rhythmic shimmer.
- **Left/Right Hand Sticking**: Real drummers switch from one-handed 8ths to alternating L/R sticking on fast 16ths (and adjust when one hand must leave the hi-hat to hit the snare on 2 and 4). Subtle alternating velocity differences or round-robin timbres emulate that physical reality.

## 3. Kick, Bass & Drum Pitch Integration

### Bass Lines and Kick Drums as a Single Composite (pp. 158–162)
- Solve low-end mud **compositionally before EQ or sidechain compression**: treat kick and bass as **one monophonic composite line** where kick and bass notes do not strike simultaneously.
- **Strict alternating**: Offbeat bass notes between 4-on-the-floor kicks (trance/commercial EDM), or syncopated bass filling rests around kicks (house/techno/funk).
- **Sustained-bass option**: When short notes sound clipped, hold longer bass notes but ensure **note onsets (attacks) never coincide** with kick transients—and watch out for long 808 kick tails colliding with sub-bass pitches.

### Drums to Pitches and Vice Versa (pp. 163–166)
- Repurpose a strong drum pattern's MIDI rhythm to trigger a bass/chord/melody instrument (transposed up and snapped to scale), or map a melodic rhythm onto drum kit pieces so rhythmic DNA unifies pitched and unpitched parts.

### Tuning Everything (pp. 167–169)
- Acoustic drums read as mostly unpitched except **toms** (often tuned to intervals of the tonic chord, e.g., kick on C, toms on E♭ and G in C minor).
- Electronic **sine-wave kicks / 808s** have unmistakable fundamental pitch—tune them to the song's key (and adapt if the song modulates). Even pitched percussion (woodblocks, cowbells) should match the key.
