# Ballad Tutti Hits, Band Kicks, Setups & Transitions

Grounded strictly in:
- Jerry Tolson, *It's All About the Rhythm (Section)* (Midwest Clinic, p. 4)
- Steve Fidyk, *Compositional Jazz Band Drumming* (Midwest Clinic, pp. 5–10)
- U.S. Navy *Musician Volume 2* (NAVEDTRA 15026C, Section 2 Ch. 6 pp. 126–128 *Unison and Octave Tutti*; Section 3 Ch. 3 p. 145)
- Frans Absil, *Arranging by Examples* (§5.4 *Percussive Voicing*, §5.5 *Tutti Voicings*, §6.1 *Bell Chords* pp. 113–114, §6.6 *Variable Density*, §11.1, §11.2.3 *Start and Stop* [Introduction & Coda], §11.2.4 *Contrasting Bridge and Transition*)

## 1. Core Definitions & Role Separation

1. **Kicks / Hits vs. Setups** (*Tolson p. 4; Fidyk pp. 7–9; US Navy p. 145*):
   - **Kick / Ensemble Figure / Hit**: A synchronized rhythmic accent struck together by the band/ensemble.
   - **Setup (Prep)**: An improvised or written drum/rhythm lead-in fill immediately **preceding** an ensemble figure or section entrance to telegraph the arrival and lock the band together.
2. **Duration Rule for Drum Kicks** (*Tolson p. 4, Item II.10*):
   - **Short kicks (`quarter note` or shorter)**: Accent with **Snare drum only** for tight, crisp punctuation.
   - **Long kicks (longer than a quarter note / sustained)**: Accent with **Bass drum and Crash cymbal together** for weight and sustain.
3. **On-the-Beat Anchor for Off-Beat Entrances** (*US Navy p. 145, Item 6*):
   - When the melody/strings/horns enter **off the beat** (anticipated push or syncopation), placing an **on-the-beat kick with snare and bass drum** helps the ensemble nail the off-beat entrance cleanly.

## 2. Three Drum Setup Strategies (*Steve Fidyk, pp. 7–9*)

Choose the setup approach based on tempo and ensemble tightness:
1. **Parallel Motion**: Fill leads directly into the ensemble figure so drums play **in rhythmic unison** with the band's hit. At slow ballad tempos (e.g., ~84 BPM), **eighth-note triplets or sixteenth-note subdivisions** work well as setup fills (*Fidyk p. 8*).
2. **Counterpoint**: Fill creates a complementary accent **"in the holes"** where the melody/ensemble rests and breathes (useful when a rhythmically tricky unison hit might sound cluttered or unstable, *Fidyk p. 9*).
3. **Connector Material**: Rhythmic subdivisions (quarters, eighths, triplets, 16ths) bridging successive ensemble phrases (`R L R L` sticking for crisper/staccato edge vs. `R R L L` for rounder/legato flow, *Fidyk p. 10*).
4. **Contour Matching** (*Fidyk pp. 5–6*): Match ascending ensemble figures by moving from low kit voices (**bass drum / floor tom**) up to higher voices (**snare / hi-hat / cymbals**).

## 3. Full-Band Coordination During Tutti & Percussive Hits

1. **Percussive Tutti Rhythm-Section Lock** (*Absil §5.4, p. 101*):
   - **Bass & Guitar**: Abandon continuous walking/strumming patterns and **lock directly onto the rhythmic accents** of the tutti figure.
   - **Piano**: Either double the full ensemble chord voicings across both hands on the hits, or insert sparse high-register fills during ensemble rests.
   - **Drums**: Prepare the articulations and support hits with toms and crash cymbals; respect sudden dynamic contrasts (e.g., `mp -> fff`) and crisp/sustained articulations (`>` vs `^`).
2. **Unison & Multi-Octave Tutti** (*US Navy pp. 126–128*):
   - Writing a thematic statement or climactic turnaround in **unison or multi-octave spread** across compatible instruments creates maximum clarity and impact, especially when juxtaposed immediately before/after thick multi-part chords (*Variable Density*, *Absil §6.6*).
   - Avoid sustained long unisons in uncomfortable extreme registers; keep accompaniment softer when a lead line enters during homophonic tutti.
3. **Bell Chords** (*Absil §6.1, pp. 113–114*):
   - Instead of striking all tutti chord notes simultaneously, stagger instrument entrances one after another (**ascending** bottom-to-top, or **descending** top-to-bottom); when staggered by constant rhythmic intervals (e.g., *Ballad q=72*, Example 6.1 p. 114), the overlapping entries can create both a blooming sustained chord and an **eighth-note rhythmic groove** of juxtaposed attacks.

## 4. Transitions, Stop Breaks, Dropouts & Key-Lift Setups

1. **Pre-Climax Stop Breaks, Dropouts & Dramatic Pauses** (*Big George Pt. 1 p. 39, Pt. 3 p. 59 [Peter Gunn], Pt. 4 p. 35 [Snake Davis]; Mix Rescue; Absil §11.1 & §11.2.4*):
   - Creating a sudden **drop in tension** at the start of the bridge (*Absil §11.1, §11.2.4*), muting drums/bass briefly right before a chorus (*Mix Rescue*), or using a **stop break / dramatic pause / big drum break** right before a chorus or key change (*Big George Pt. 1, Pt. 3, Pt. 4*) amplifies the emotional explosion when the full band re-enters.
   - *Rhythmic Breaks Between Vocal Sentences* (*Absil §11.1, p. 194*): Close vocal phrases in Chorus 2 with short, punctuated ensemble breaks + drum accents during vocal rests.
2. **Introductions, Endings & Key-Lift Build-Ups** (*Absil §11.2.3 *Start and Stop* [Intro & Coda] pp. 209–217 & §11.2.4; Big George Pt. 1; Dave Stewart Pt. 4; HK EDB*):
   - Open ballads with either a **riff/vamp intro** (steady groove) or a **build-up intro** (crescendo launching the main theme, *Absil §11.2.3*).
   - For mid-song transitions and bridges (*Absil §11.2.4*), combine a dynamic crescendo (`mf -> ff`), a **chromatically ascending bassline**, and rising subdivisions (e.g., ascending scale runs or tom/snare builds) to launch into a new chorus or a **whole-tone (`+2` semitone) key change** (*Big George Pt. 1*, up a tone around 2/3 through, at most once per song; half-step `+1` lifts are also common in ballad production per *Stewart Pt. 4* and *pop-and-ballad.md*).
