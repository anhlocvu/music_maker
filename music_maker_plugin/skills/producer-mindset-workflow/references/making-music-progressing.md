# Creative Producer Strategies: Problems of Progressing (Dennis DeSantis, *Making Music*)

*Distilled faithfully from Dennis DeSantis, "Making Music: 74 Creative Strategies for Electronic Music Producers" (Part 2: Problems of Progressing, pp. 91–257). Rhythm/beat strategies (pp. 120–169) live in `groove-rhythm-drums`; melody/harmony strategies (pp. 178–237) live in `music-theory-harmony`.*

## 1. Structural Expansion & Depth

### Breadth Before Depth (pp. 92–94)
- **Problem**: Obsessing over perfecting one kick drum or synth patch early stalls the entire song before sections even exist.
- **Solution**: Build **horizontal breadth first**—sketch rough placeholders across all sections (intro, verse, chorus, outro) before zooming in on micro-detail mixing or sound-design polish.

### Foreground, Middle Ground, and Background (pp. 95–98)
- **Problem**: Music feels flat and two-dimensional.
- **Solution**: Assign every part a clear perceptual depth plane *during composition*:
  - **Foreground**: Detailed, prominent lead/hook elements (dry/close, articulated, center/spotlight).
  - **Middle Ground**: Supporting groove, chords, rhythm guitar, bass pocket.
  - **Background**: Subtle atmosphere, pads, distant textures, reverb tails—kept simpler and softer so foreground shines.

### Fuzzy Boundaries (pp. 99–103)
- **Problem**: DAW block/clip arrangements cut cleanly on barlines like vertical walls, sounding stiff and mechanical.
- **Solution**: Make section transitions jagged by:
  - **Extending** 1–2 tracks' tails past the barline into the next section,
  - **Retracting / leading in** 1–2 tracks slightly early before the boundary, or
  - **Inserting deliberate silence** (1–2 beats) in select tracks right before the drop/chorus.

### Maximal Density (pp. 244–246)
- **Problem**: You finished Section A and feel paralyzed trying to invent Section B from scratch.
- **Solution**: Build a hypothetical **maximally dense climax section** containing every instrument and idea playing together; then construct all other sections by subtracting combinations of layers from that core.

### Dramatic Arc (pp. 254–257)
- **Problem**: Sections feel pasted together without narrative momentum.
- **Solution**: Shape the whole timeline along **Freytag's Pyramid**:
  1. **Exposition**: Introduce core motifs, timbre palette, and harmonic frame.
  2. **Rising Action**: Mutate motifs, add layers, shorten harmonic/rhythmic intervals to raise tension.
  3. **Climax**: Peak textural density, register width, and energy (e.g., final chorus or drop).
  4. **Falling Action**: Strip layers, widen space, relax tension.
  5. **Dénouement**: Resolved outro or quiet echo of exposition.

## 2. Systematic Variation & Humanization

### Creating Variation 1–3 (pp. 104–119)
- **1. Mutation of Clones**: Duplicate a seed bar/loop 8 times; alter **only one meaningful parameter** per copy (rhythm, pitch, density, timbre, articulation), creating an evolving family.
- **2. Mutation Over Generations**: Make 1 duplicate, change one thing, then duplicate the *edited* version for the next change—allowing the idea to drift organically far from its origin.
- **3. Note Transformations**: Apply systematic MIDI/note operations (transposition, inversion, retrograde, rhythmic augmentation/diminution, velocity scaling, octave displacement) so variations stay structurally tied to the motif.

### Silence and Noise (pp. 170–172)
- Pay attention to the **spaces between notes**: subtle room tone, reverb decay, vinyl/ambient noise floor, or deliberate dry rests shape perceived continuity and contrast as much as active notes.

### Sampling the Old-Fashioned Way (pp. 173–177)
- Disable automatic pitch-preserving time-stretching when repurposing loops/samples so tempo shifts naturally alter pitch and timbre (classic breakbeat/hip-hop artifacts).

### The Rhythm of Automation & Humanizing with Envelopes (pp. 238–243)
- Move beyond slow linear filter sweeps: shape parameter/velocity/dynamic envelopes **rhythmically** (stepped, syncopated, cycling against the beat) and add subtle per-note variations in tuning, release length, and velocity so electronic lines breathe like live players.

### Deliberately Bad Listening (pp. 247–249)
- Test your work on terrible speakers, from an adjacent room, or at ultra-low volume during composition: weak balances, overly long sections, and missing hooks jump out immediately when fine detail disappears.

### Randomness and Responsibility (pp. 250–253)
- Generative/random tools are idea sparks, never finished decisions. Listen critically to every generated bar, keep only what serves the song, and throw away the rest.
